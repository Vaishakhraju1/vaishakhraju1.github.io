CAE AUTOMATION · CONNECTOR STIFFNESS CALIBRATION

Automated Abaqus model
calibration using Python.

Developed an iterative Python–Abaqus workflow to calibrate a finite-element model against a prescribed displacement target. It connects input-file modification, analysis execution, ODB results extraction, and parameter updates in a repeatable computational process.

The script updates connector stiffness under *Connector Elasticity, component=1, runs a new analysis, and reads the maximum nodal displacement magnitude from the last available frame of the selected step. It then adjusts the stiffness and repeats, seeking a response within the target tolerance.

PythonAbaqusParameter calibrationSimulation automation
PRESCRIBED TARGET4.0 ± 0.1

Maximum nodal displacement magnitude
in the model’s consistent displacement units.

Stopping criterion · not a reported result

Engineering contribution

Designed the parameter-update logic and integrated model editing, solver execution, response extraction, and convergence checks. The workflow automates the repeated changes and reruns needed for simulation-driven parameter calibration.

PARAMETER-UPDATE LOGIC

target = 4.0
tolerance = 0.1
stiffness_step = 5.0

# Inside the simulation loop:
if abs(max_disp - target) <= tolerance:
    break

if max_disp > target:
    stiffness += stiffness_step
else:
    stiffness -= stiffness_step

Adapted excerpt with variable names clarified. Model identifiers, file paths, and job-management code are omitted.

Method & validation scope +

The calibrated parameter is connector stiffness. The iteration is controlled by Python around successive Abaqus analyses; it does not modify Abaqus’s internal solver. The separate GitHub calibration example uses Young’s modulus and bracketed bisection on a synthetic axial-bar model.

This research script uses fixed stiffness increments. Maximum iteration limits, stiffness bounds, and protection against oscillation remain development improvements. The supplied code establishes the implemented method, but does not by itself establish convergence or a validated calibration result.

The later workflow extracts the initial coordinates and nodal displacements, reconstructs the deformed endpoints, and calculates the change in their separation. This gives muscle elongation from the geometry of the two endpoints.

Development results & scope +

The January 2025 development record documents a ScanIP model with two material regions exported as a single instance. A recorded iteration sequence settled near an elongation of 4.898 mm and a signed connector force of −20.115 N before the run was manually stopped. These are results from that specific test, rather than a measured biological muscle property or a validated bite-force prediction.

The work also explored fitted force–length curves. A higher-gain update produced oscillating, excessive forces, making numerical stability and the choice of force–length relation clear priorities for further development.

NONLINEAR FEA & MATERIALS TESTING · NIT KARNATAKA · PhD

Knee mechanics and implant
material performance.

Combined finite element modelling with laboratory testing to investigate knee load transfer, implant geometry, and wear resistance of UHMWPE bearing material.

Engineering problem

Cartilage structure and material assumptions affect predicted joint stresses. Prosthetic bearing geometry and material processing also influence contact response and wear. The work examined these factors through complementary computational and experimental studies.

My contribution

Developed graded and heterogeneous cartilage models, investigated collagen-fibre orientation for intact and osteoarthritic knees, assessed changes to prosthetic bearing geometry, and developed a patterned irradiation approach for modifying UHMWPE properties.

AbaqusFEBioNonlinear contactConstitutive modellingTribologyExperimental testing
01 / MATERIAL MODELLING

Compare cartilage representations

Compared homogeneous and heterogeneous material models, including poroelastic and fibre-reinforced behaviour, under joint loading.

02 / DESIGN ANALYSIS

Evaluate load transfer

Examined contact pressure, stress, strain, and displacement during gait, and the sensitivity of prosthetic response to curvature changes.

03 / PHYSICAL TESTING

Characterise bearing material

Evaluated patterned gamma irradiation using reciprocating wear tests, hardness measurements, dynamic mechanical analysis, and FTIR.

Key findings

  • Heterogeneous cartilage models predicted lower peak contact pressure than homogeneous representations in the studied comparisons, showing why material-model selection matters.
  • Collagen-fibre orientation and loading direction changed the predicted response. The intact-versus-osteoarthritic comparison depended on activity, as detailed in the walking and running simulations below.
  • Samples irradiated through a 1 mm hole pattern showed lower wear than fully exposed samples in the reported tests. Wear performance depended on irradiation dose; increasing dose did not consistently improve it.

PhD / GAIT SIMULATIONS

Walking and running gait simulations

Simulated the stance phase of walking and running to investigate how cartilage structure and collagen-fibre orientation affect knee-joint mechanics.

Simulation setup

Used an Open Knee tibiofemoral model in FEBio with fibre-reinforced porous hyperelastic cartilage. Applied literature-derived force components and rotations for the two activities, comparing four intact and three osteoarthritic cartilage models.

Engineering contribution

Implemented different collagen-fibre orientations and examined contact pressure, maximum principal elastic stress, Lagrange strain, fluid pressure, and fibre stretch across stance. This connected material structure and loading history to the predicted joint response.

FEBioWalking & runningGait loadingFibre-reinforced cartilageIntact & osteoarthritic models

The panels show intact model-1. Colour scales differ between walking and running; compare the labelled values rather than colour alone.

What the simulations showed
  • Activity and fibre orientation changed the predicted mechanical response. Parallel-oriented models showed higher maximum stresses and fluid pressure than proximal–distal-oriented models in both gait analyses.
  • The intact-versus-osteoarthritic comparison depended on the activity: Chapter 6 reports greater maximum contact pressure in intact models during walking, and in osteoarthritic models during running.
  • During running, maximum principal Lagrange strain followed similar trends in intact and osteoarthritic models, with higher values for parallel-oriented fibres than for perpendicular or inclined orientations.
Gait workflow & modelling scope +

The analysis used published gait inputs and Open Knee geometry, rather than newly recorded patient-specific gait measurements. The simulations examine tissue and loading assumptions; they do not establish a patient-specific clinical prediction. Source: PhD thesis, Chapter 6, especially Figures 6.1, 6.4, and 6.5 and Section 6.8.

Technical scope & thesis source +

The computational work used Abaqus and FEBio, comparing constitutive assumptions and tissue response in tibiofemoral and prosthetic knee models. The experimental work used medical-grade UHMWPE, irradiation doses of 30, 100, and 150 kGy, and patterned, top-only, and full-exposure configurations.

Wear testing used a reciprocating ball-on-disc configuration with a 40 N normal load. These are specimen-level laboratory findings; testing actual tibial inserts in a knee simulator was identified as future work.

Source: Kinetics/Kinematics of Intact and Arthritic Knee Cartilages and a Novel Approach to Enhance Wear Characteristics of UHMWPE Tibial Inserts for Prosthetic Knee, Vaishakh R, NIT Karnataka. Thesis submitted October 2022; PhD awarded 2023. Main material-modelling findings: Chapters 5–7; experimental methods and results: Chapter 8.

ROTATING MACHINERY · CONDITION MONITORING · SVNIT SURAT · M.TECH · 2015

Diagnosing bearing faults
and shaft bend.

Used vibration measurements, FFT and envelope spectra to identify rolling-bearing defects and bent-shaft signatures on a controlled machine-fault test rig.

Engineering problem

Different faults can produce overlapping vibration signatures. Reliable diagnosis requires linking measured peaks and harmonics to machine speed, bearing geometry, fault location, and the direction of measurement.

My contribution

Conducted experiments on healthy and faulty bearings and a bent shaft, collected vibration data in multiple directions, compared measured defect frequencies with theoretical predictions, and investigated fault severity and critical speed.

01 / BEARING DIAGNOSIS

Identify defect components

Distinguished outer-race, inner-race, rolling-element, and cage faults using characteristic frequencies and their harmonics.

02 / FAULT SEVERITY

Compare controlled defects

Used envelope spectra to assess bearing balls with different defect sizes, including defects introduced by EDM.

03 / SHAFT DYNAMICS

Investigate bend and critical speed

Examined 1×/2× rotational components and directional vibration response; found that rotor-disc position affected critical speed.

Test equipment & thesis source +

The study used a PRUFTECHNIK VIB 2.20 machine-fault simulator, SKF 1205 EKTN9 bearings, a VIBXPERT II two-channel vibration analyser, and a VIB 6.142 R accelerometer. Measurements covered healthy bearings, individual and combined bearing defects, and bent-shaft configurations.

Source: Vibration Based Condition Monitoring of Bearing Defects and Shaft Bend, Vaishakh R, SVNIT Surat, 2014–2015. Experimental setup: Chapter 3; frequency comparisons and shaft tests: Chapter 4; conclusions: Chapter 5.
